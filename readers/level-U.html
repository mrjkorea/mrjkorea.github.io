5Records could be made more quickly. A stone stela of one pharaoh still shows this carved writing. The Cup of Immortality came from a pharaoh's tomb. As years passed, people used hieroglyphs less and less. By about 30 BCE, stone hieroglyphs served mainly as decoration. They were no longer mainly a way to share messages. A person today can see real hieroglyphs without a trip to Egypt. Major museums in big cities display Egyptian writing. In some cities the signs are right on the street. The Place de la Concorde stands in Paris, France. The Luxor Obelisk rises there. The obelisk is three thousand years old. Glyphs on it tell about the rule of Ramses II. Egypt gave the obelisk to France in 1833. The gift came from Egypt's government. Far away, a wall in Palenque, Mexico, is covered with glyphs. Palenque is a preserved city of the ancient Maya. Egyptian hieroglyphs were fading when another picture system was growing.

6Home for the ancient Maya lay on the world's far side from Egypt. Their descendants still live in Mesoamerica. That region is now southern Mexico. It also includes northern Central America. The countries there include Guatemala. They include Belize. They include Honduras. They include El Salvador. Among picture languages, this one ranks with the most complex. Scholars think the Maya first used this writing around 1000 BCE. The oldest examples that survive date only to 300 BCE. The Maya, like the Egyptians, called writing a gift from the gods. Itzamna was the supreme deity. He was a shaman. He created writing. Because the writing was holy, only certain people could use it. Specially chosen scribes could read it. They could write it. Royalty could read it. Royalty could write it too. The ancient Mayan system was like the Egyptian one in important ways. The Maya used about 800 glyphs. Those glyphs split into two groups.

7Most were logograms. A logogram stood for a whole word. About 150 other glyphs were syllables. Writers could use those syllables as prefixes. They could use them as suffixes. The Maya had no alphabet of single sounds. They did not give each sound its own glyph. Mayan words were built by grouping glyphs inside a square cartouche. Glyphs that belonged together were gathered inside that square. The cluster worked a bit like the words in a sentence. The squares sat on a grid. Writers placed them in paired columns. A reader went from left to right. The reader then moved down in a zigzag. Mayan numbers use three symbols. One symbol is a shell shape. Another symbol is a dot. The third symbol is a bar. Inside a Mayan temple, a cartouche holding calendar glyphs was uncovered. The temple is in Palenque, Mexico. Scribes took great care with each beautiful cartouche. The design of a cartouche was a point of pride.

8A scribe wanted the work to stand out. Glyphs were arranged in the way the scribe thought best. Each message became beautiful in its own way. The same idea could be shown in more than one arrangement. That freedom made reading a bit harder. An urn shows Itzamna. He is the Mayan god of creation. Mayan scribes mattered to tribal kings. Egyptian scribes had mattered to the pharaoh in a similar way. Mayan scribes wrote words for religious ceremonies. They used brushes made from porcupine quills. The glyphs went into books of deerskin. Writing often recorded a king's wins in battle. Stone walls of monuments carried carved glyphs. Temple walls carried them. Obelisks carried them. Propaganda is the right name for messages of this sort. They told of a ruler's successes. The displays let rulers show power. Scribes prepared the art. They prepared the words. That work praised the king. A large share of Mayan documents dealt with timekeeping.

9The Maya were skilled astronomers. Their calendar held 365 days. The days were split into eighteen months. Each of those months had 20 days. Five days were left over. Those five days were called wayeb. People believed the wayeb days were unlucky. Scribes had the job of posting these messages in public. For a scribe of a winning king, the job was easy. It was also safe. A scribe on the losing side of a battle had a harder time. A war fought with words was one the conquerors meant to win. They took away the enemy's chance to use words. Hands of captured scribes on the losing side were often damaged. The hurt was meant to stop them from writing ever again. Strong examples of Mayan glyphs remain across the Yucatan Peninsula. Ruins at Palenque carry the marks of ancient scribes. Ruins at Teotihuacan carry them. Ruins at Tikal carry them.

10Like the hieroglyphs of Egypt, these glyphs tell stories. The stories are about people who lived long ago. The beautiful picture language of the ancient Maya is no longer used. Egyptian picture writing is no longer used either. Other ancient picture languages are still in use. Chinese is one of them. Japanese is one of them. Korean is one of them. Each of these Asian languages uses thousands of picture symbols. Picture signs exist in modern life too. Signs for travel between countries use simple glyphs built as blocks. The signs point travelers to their bags. They point travelers to a bus. The travelers may come from any part of the world. Glyphs are easier than signs written in many languages. A single picture can still do the work of a dozen words. That was true in the past. It is true now. Travelers around the world can share meaning through these common symbols.
