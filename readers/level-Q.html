1. A Keeper's Work with Zoo Animals

1The main gate leads into the Cleveland Metroparks Zoo. Jeff Polcen works there as a lead zookeeper. An interviewer asks how this work first caught his interest. Jeff says his earlier job was with the Metroparks. In Cleveland, Ohio, the city parks go by the name Metroparks. He worked at the stables. Then he heard that the zoo had an open job. His first zoo post was in the Birds of the World building. Later he moved to the African animal area. He has stayed in that area for 17 years. Jeff grew up on a farm. Hoofed animals have always been the ones he likes. Giraffes are relatives of cows and of horses. Zebras are relatives of them too. The way these animals act is much the same. Their body build is much the same too. Even so, many differences are interesting. Jeff says each day teaches him something new. One photo shows a friendly zebra. Jeff Polcen gives it a scratch behind the ears. The interviewer asks which skills a keeper needs. Jeff says the skill that matters most is careful watching. A keeper looks at a whole group of animals. The keeper then works out what is going on.

2The animals might be getting sick. One of them might be about to give birth. The group might not be getting along. Wild animals like these can be dangerous. Common sense has to guide every keeper. The interviewer asks which animals are in Jeff's care. Jeff says his title is lead keeper. That title means he supervises and still does the daily work. He watches over the African savannah. He also watches the area with rhinos and monkey island. Many animals are under his care. Giraffes live there. Zebras live there. Antelope live there. Ostriches live there. Vultures live there. Native African birds live there too. The zoo runs mixed species exhibits. Birds and mammals share one yard. They share it only when that mix is safe. A photo shows Jeff caring for zebras and for many other animals. An adult female giraffe is named Bridgit. She stands beside her son Mac. Bridgit is a Masai giraffe. Only about 50 Masai giraffes live in North America. The interviewer asks what Jeff likes most in this job. Jeff says he can work on his own. Nobody stands over him and gives orders. In zoo work, each person finds the post that fits best.

3Some people like working with the monkeys. Some people like working with the birds. Hoofed animals are the ones Jeff likes best. Many giraffe babies have been born at this zoo. Working with those babies is a joy for him. The interviewer asks if Jeff grows close to each animal, the way a person does with a pet. Jeff says it is hard not to grow fond of some animals. Care at zoos has moved forward a great deal. Because of that, animals live longer than they once did. As an animal gets older, Jeff sees illness start. He also sees other problems that come with age. Then he must decide how to care for that older animal. Sometimes the animal is suffering. The kindest step then is to put it to sleep. A photo shows Bridgit at eighteen years old. The interviewer asks about the scariest moment in Jeff's work. Jeff says a giraffe ran him over. The keepers were bringing the giraffes inside. One female spun around. She ran back toward him. He had almost no time to turn. He tried to move out of her path. She hit him. She went over him.

4She came down on him. The interviewer asks if he was hurt. Jeff says the hurt was small. Mostly he was just sore. A hoof landed on him a few times. Nothing major came from it. The interviewer asks what a giraffe weighs. Jeff says the weight is 1,500 to 2,500 pounds. That equals 680 to 1,134 kilograms. The number depends on whether the giraffe is male or female. Giraffes are huge. A photo shows a group of giraffes. The interviewer asks how Jeff handles a group like that. He asks if the keepers move the animals one by one. Jeff says they do not. Herd life is the pattern for most of the hoofed stock. A herd does each thing together. Once the animals learn a routine, they stick to it. A keeper has to stick to that routine too. A change in the routine confuses the herd. Each giraffe knows the stall it should enter. Keepers call out orders. Sometimes a keeper taps an animal with a long stick. The stick lets the keeper stay a safe distance away. The interviewer asks how the animals get medical care. He asks if a large animal gets a shot and sleeps while the work is done.

5Jeff says the vets handle most of the big animals in one way. For most big animals, anesthesia begins with a blowpipe or an air gun. If the work takes a long time, gas keeps the animal under. Anesthesia for a giraffe is risky. The team must be sure the procedure is truly needed. Every needed person must be ready. Every needed piece of equipment must be ready. Complications can come up. A photo shows a zookeeper training a rhinoceros to take a shot. A giraffe's health takes a great deal of watching. The interviewer asks what a giraffe does under anesthesia. Jeff says the team watches the way the animal goes down. They do not want the head to strike anything. A giraffe on its side may struggle to breathe. The body is very heavy. Sometimes the anesthesia makes the neck muscles hard to move. The team thinks the plan through before any procedure. A photo shows a young giraffe at rest. The interviewer asks whether giraffes ever lie down. Jeff says they do. Every night they lie down. Their legs stay tucked under the body. They do not sleep for hours the way people do.

6A nap lasts ten to fifteen minutes. Then the giraffe gets up again. The interviewer asks if the head stays up. Jeff says the head often rests on the hip. A giraffe does that for a deep sleep. The interviewer says the pose sounds curled. A sight like that must be amazing. Jeff agrees. All of them lying down is a neat sight. He sees it when he arrives in the morning. The interviewer asks Jeff for a funny zoo story. Jeff tells about a winter a few years ago. Ostriches were out in the yard. They were on an icy patch. Jeff was trying to bring them in. They had lain down. The ice was too slick for them to stand again. By the doorway, melting had left one strip firm enough for walking. Jeff sat with his back to the fence. He set his feet on each ostrich. He shoved each bird the way a player shoves a hockey puck. The birds slid across the ice. They slid well over to the thawed spot. Then Jeff prodded them to a place where they could stand. Then he led the birds inside. Photos show ostriches.

7One photo shows a mother ostrich guarding her young. The interviewer asks if ostriches are dangerous. Jeff says breeding season is when the males turn dangerous. A kick from a male can kill. Female ostriches are usually calm. At times they are still a challenge. It depends on the way you work with them. It also depends on the bond you build. Any animal can be dangerous. That is when careful watching matters. A keeper watches what is happening. Then the keeper knows when to leave the area. Each animal has its own personality. The interviewer asks what Jeff thinks about cages. Jeff says zoos have traveled far from the days of iron bars. Most exhibits at his zoo are interactive now. Staff work hard so animals can act in natural ways. Those ways keep the animals from growing bored. The yards of the exhibits are bigger now. The yards hold what keepers call furniture. Rocks are one kind of furniture. Logs are another kind. Most zoos have a team for this job. The team builds places that interest the animals. Zoos pass ideas to one another. Zoo magazines report a test. They name the animal in the test.

8They say the test worked out well. Workers who care for animals have a strong network. A photo shows pandas playing on exhibit furniture. Another photo shows an adult slender-horned gazelle. The interviewer asks which animal is hardest to care for, and why. Jeff names the slender-horned gazelle. This animal comes from North Africa. Few of them live in captivity. They are inbred. That means babies have been born to close relatives. So the immune system is weaker than it should be. Common infections reach them more easily. Day to day, they are easy to manage. Even so, medical problems come up at times. The interviewer asks which animal in Jeff's care is the most dangerous. Jeff says any hoofed animal can be. Among them, giraffes are the most dangerous. A kick from a giraffe can be the worst. Right now Jeff keeps four giraffes. The number here has been as high as eight. Calves were part of that count. A photo shows a giraffe walking through its exhibit. In a birthing stall, a mother giraffe stands with her calf. At birth, the baby drops 1.8 meters. That drop is 6 feet. Sand fills the bottom of the stall.

9Straw lies over the sand. The sand and straw soften the fall. The interviewer asks if Jeff has a special story. Jeff tells about a giraffe calf the team had to deliver in 1998. This birth was the mother's third or fourth pregnancy. This time she was in trouble. The calf had stopped moving along the birth canal. Jeff reached inside her. He helped pull until the head was out. Then the vet and Jeff pulled her the rest of the way. Inside the birth canal, her supply of air must have stopped. They got her breathing. For a few days, the baby did not want to nurse. The keepers left her with her mom. They also fed her through a tube. On the third day, she began to nurse by herself. She grew into a really nice animal. She formed a bond with the keepers too. Handling her was really easy because of that bond. She lives in Kansas City now. There, she is one of the best giraffes. Her age now is almost four. A photo shows a zookeeper feeding a young giraffe. The interviewer says that calf taught Jeff something. A baby handled that much at the start becomes much easier to manage.

10He asks why the zoo does not raise every baby that way. He wants each calf to become that easy to handle. Jeff says hand raising happens only when it absolutely must. An animal raised by its mother is better. It learns how to act like the animal it is. An animal aimed at humans forgets that. A zebra may stop acting like a zebra. A giraffe may stop acting like a giraffe. A photo shows this baby giraffe running through its new surroundings. A photo shows Jeff hand-feeding a friendly giraffe. The interviewer asks if this work will last for all of Jeff's working years. Jeff says he thinks he will. He has had many successes here. A lot still remains that he wants to finish. Caring for these animals feels very satisfying. The work is interesting. The work is challenging too. Giraffes usually eat while they stand. Now and then they must bend down for a drink. The move down takes a long time. They inch downward in a slow way. They spread their long legs so they can balance. In the wild, that pose leaves them very open to danger.

2. Amelia Earhart Takes to the Sky

1A photo shows Amelia in 1937. This book tells how flying machines began. It tells about her youth in Kansas. It tells about the years she spent in the air. It tells about her last flight. Success would make her the first woman to do it. It would also make her the first person to cross the Atlantic twice by airplane. Before any of that, she had to succeed. The trip was full of danger. Planes of the 1930s were small. They lacked the instruments that pilots use today.

2A pilot had to guide the plane by sight. She also had to hold the plane under tight control. Those black clouds left no room to fly around them. So Amelia aimed the little plane straight into them. Ice locked the controls. The plane dropped into a tailspin. Amelia fought to get the plane back. At last the ice melted. She brought the plane out of the spin. Many hours later, the plane came down in Northern Ireland. The Atlantic now lay behind her. She had been the one at the controls. Fame was already hers. From then on, people would call her a legend. The thing people would remember most was still ahead. The flight that made her vanish was an attempt to circle the world. Reporters crowd around Amelia after that Atlantic flight. A photo shows the Wright brothers on their first flight. The first airplane flight in the world took place in 1903. Two brothers got a winged machine into the air in North Carolina.

3The brothers were Wilbur and Orville Wright. Many people found the idea of flying exciting. Danger was part of it too. The planes were fragile. The planes were slow. Bad weather was hard on them. Crashes killed many pilots. By 1937, planes were much better. Engineers had designed stronger airplanes. Crashes still took place. Her childhood already pointed toward the things she would later do. 4One day she went to an air show. The planes filled her with wonder. They flew in loops. They did stunts. Right then, flying became what she wanted to do. Her parents gave help. She took lessons in piloting. She saved enough money to buy a plane. Soon she herself was flying in air shows. Her work often meant helping children. Later she moved to Boston. George Putnam worked as a publicist. He was looking for a woman for a special ride. The goal was a first: a woman riding a plane across the Atlantic. The plan was dangerous. The year before, three women had died while trying that crossing. Up to that time, every Atlantic crossing by airplane had been made by a man.

5Anyone who tried it had to face danger. Fatigue had to be faced too. George believed Amelia Earhart was the right choice. She wanted to go. In her lifetime, Amelia held many jobs. In those years, a job for a woman was unusual. She worked as a nurse. She worked as a social worker. She worked as a pilot. She worked as a clothing designer. She worked as a magazine editor. She worked as a writer. She worked as an airline executive. She worked as a professor. Women pilots formed the Ninety-Nines with her help. That group is made up of women who fly. The group is still active today. A photo from 1918 shows Amelia in a nurse's uniform. On the 1928 flight, she rode only as a passenger. Nothing about her life stayed the same after that. Headlines around the world named the first woman to cross the Atlantic. After the flight, fame followed her.

6She kept busy writing books. She gave talks about flying. She designed clothes for active women like herself. She designed luggage for them too. She even helped start two airlines. Later she became the wife of George Putnam. Flying stayed part of her life. Southampton, England, is where a photo places her after the Atlantic flight of 1928. At thirty-nine, only one major flight was still undone. The last great test was a flight around the world. Only one pilot had flown around the world before. That pilot did not follow the equator. The equator is the longest path. It is also the hardest path. Amelia chose that path. Would she make it? At that point, people trusted her skill in the air. On May 21, 1937, she left with her navigator. His name was Fred Noonan. The trip from California ended in Florida. The plane was a Lockheed Electra. Before the world flight, she poses on the Lockheed.

7A map shows the 1937 route. The finished part passes Miami. It continues through South America and Africa. Asia is on the route as well. A gap marks the area where the plane vanished. The unfinished part of the route is marked too. Amelia and Fred made their first Electra stop in Florida. The new plane had already crossed the whole North American continent. South America was the next land on the route. After that they flew to Africa. Then the route went to India. Then it went to Asia. Then it went to Australia. It looked as if Amelia would again finish a historic flight. Nothing seemed wrong. New Guinea was their stop on June 29, 1937. New Guinea lies just north of Australia. There they put fuel in the plane. They checked the maps. The next takeoff gave no sign of trouble ahead. A map marks Asia, India, Hawaii, Lae, Howland Island, and Australia.

8Indonesia is the setting of a photo from one of their final stops, with Fred Noonan and Amelia. As the plane neared Howland Island, the radio messages turned odd. 9No trace of the plane turned up. No trace of Amelia turned up. No trace of Fred turned up. After a year of looking, her husband reached a belief. He believed she had died at sea. No one can be sure what took place. The 20th century counts her among America's greatest people. She let everyone see that women did not have to live inside limits.

10She let people see that the sky was open to anyone. She was a legend. A photo shows Amelia doing the thing she loved most, which was flying. Some of them measure, and some of them record. A legend is a famous person remembered for a special skill or a great deed. A navigator sets the path so a traveler can reach a chosen place. Pilots are the people who fly aircraft. Some pilots fly spacecraft. A tailspin is a fast drop. The diving plane turns round and round as it falls.

3. Bursts of Color in the Sky

1A tiny island was the first place to celebrate. The island is in the Pacific Ocean. Then Earth turned. Midnight arrived in Sydney, Australia. Fire in rings burst above the city. They burst above the city's famous opera house. Firecrackers popped in Hong Kong. Skyrockets flew over Bombay. They flew over the pyramids in Egypt. They flew over Moscow. The Eiffel Tower sent bright sparks from its top. The sparks looked like foam shooting from a bottle of champagne. Mexico City glowed red. It also glowed gold. It also glowed green. Chicago glowed in those same colors. Fireworks marked the new year in places all over the world. Fireworks join science with art. That mix creates displays among the loveliest ever made. Making fireworks and launching them has a name. The name is pyrotechnics. The word is said PIE-roe-TECK-nicks. In most cases, people still do this dangerous craft by hand. Families pass secret skills down to the next family. They also pass secret formulas down. The skills move from generation to generation. Fireworks have lit the sky by the Statue of Liberty. No one is sure when the first fireworks shot into the sky. We do know the place. It was China. It was more than 1,000 years ago.

2A legend tells of a Chinese cook. He mixed ingredients in his kitchen. By accident, he created gunpowder. A fireworks display lights a park in Xi'an. Xi'an is in Shaanxi, China. People in China stuffed this explosive black powder into tubes. Some tubes were bamboo. Some tubes were paper. Fire then blasted out of those tubes. Roman candles send fire out in a similar way today. Someone learned a new trick. A wooden carving could sit on top of the gunpowder. The carving would then shoot into the sky. One carving was a dragon. Even the dragon's mouth held gunpowder. As it flew, it seemed to spray fire. The secret of gunpowder moved on from China. It reached countries in the Middle East. Then it reached Europe. People mostly used it in weapons. Those weapons fired cannons. They fired guns. Armies often shot their cannons after a victory. The noise was a celebration. Different countries began to mix chemicals into the gunpowder. The mixes made brighter flashes. They made louder bangs. They made color. Colorful night shows soon brought the Italians fame. The Grucci family continues that Italian tradition. The family now lives in the United States. A Grucci display once lit the sky in Las Vegas, Nevada.

3Every firework starts with gunpowder. Makers want color. To get color, makers blend special chemicals into the gunpowder. These chemicals burn with flames of different colors. Kinds of salt make up most of the chemicals. That fact surprises many people. Flakes of metal may go into the powder too. When the powder explodes, the flakes become glowing hot. They create a shower of sparks. Each chemical burns in its own color. Copper is the metal in pennies. Copper burns blue-green. Pennies change toward that color when they turn green. The change in a penny is much slower. The Statue of Liberty is made of copper. That is why it looks green. A chart lists more chemicals and the colors they make. Copper salts make blue. Strontium salts make red. Sodium makes yellow. Barium nitrate makes green. Potassium perchlorate makes loud booms. Fireworks make sound because of intense heat. The heat makes the air around the firework expand. The air moves out as a wave. Your ear catches that wave. You hear it as a loud sound. Sometimes your chest can feel the wave. Your feet can feel it too. The wave is vibrating.

4Lightning makes thunder in this same way. Whistles come from hot gases. Combustion creates those gases. Tiny whistles of paper go inside the shells. Hot gases from burning stars pass through those whistles. That blowing makes the sound. Makers roll colored gunpowder into small balls. Sparkling gunpowder can be rolled the same way. The balls are called stars. A single color is enough for some stars. Other stars are layered with different colors. The stars go inside shells made of stiff paper. A single shell can carry hundreds of stars. One choice is to set the stars in a globe. Then the firework explodes as a globe of fire. The maker may pack them in a star shape. Then it explodes into a star. The maker may pack them in a smiley-face shape. Then it explodes into a smiley face. A firework has several parts. It has a leader. It has a shell. It has a time fuse. It has separate stars. The stars can hold many colors. They can hold patterns. They can hold charges that make noise. Each star may sit in a paper tube of its own. Those tubes drive the stars outward from the main firework. They leave streaks of light.

5Powder may be packed solid through the whole shell. That powder creates a single flash. It also creates a huge bang. This kind of blast is called a salute. A ball of gunpowder sits in the middle of each shape. That ball is called a bursting charge. The bursting charge blows apart in the way a firecracker does. It lights the stars. It sends the stars out into the sky. Black powder is part of the firework. Time fuses light the bursting charge. The decorative explosions follow. A leader fuse burns fast. It lights the lift charge. 6The flame dies once the oxygen is all gone. Oxygen sits inside gunpowder itself. That oxygen makes gunpowder extra flammable. Combustion also makes the nearby air expand. 7First, everything must be drawn on a diagram. Shells must fire in one planned order. The setup must make each shell go in turn. Workers start by building batteries. A battery here is a wooden rack. It shoots the shell. A cannon shoots a cannonball in the same kind of way. All the mortars are numbered. The numbers follow the diagram. The finished shells are numbered too. 8Workers load fireworks into a launcher. Everyone checks the fuse connections. They check where the shells sit. They check where the mortars sit. They check the firing order. After the switch flips, the computer is in charge. The computer begins lighting matches. Color bursts across the sky as the show begins. Sound fills the air too. The designer imagines a purple fountain at one moment. A golden blast might fit another moment.

9Flashes and crashes might fit the sound. The panel is set so shells light along with the music. A millennium celebration in Washington, D.C., used a show like this. Fireworks explode. A sparkler can send a spark into someone's eye. The spark is metal. The metal is glowing hot. Fireworks stored against the law can cause devastating fires. Fireworks also cause smoke. They cause noise. They often terrify animals. They alarm people. Many states have laws that stop people from using fireworks. The aim of those laws is to protect people. 10Never go closer to the fireworks. Never pick up leftover pieces. Those pieces may still hold explosive gunpowder. Bring protection for your ears.
