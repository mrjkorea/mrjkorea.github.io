Sound All Around

12 pages · photo · narrator: Professor — An explainer would tell this aloud, walking readers through how sound is made, travels, and is heard.

What Makes Sound? Crash, boom, bam, bang, swoosh, and splash —sounds are all around us.

People, animals, and objects make sounds. Even the weather makes sound. Sounds can be loud or soft. They can be pleasant or unpleasant.

Animals use sound in many ways. Humans use sound to communicate their thoughts and feelings. Other animals use sound to communicate, too. They use sound to defend themselves, attract a mate, or signal a warning.

Humans have to make sounds. Drums, bagpipes, instruments make music. Horns, bells, and sirens sound warnings or call people to events. Alarm clocks that it is time to get out of bed.

What Is Sound? Sound is made when an object vibrates, or moves back and forth very quickly. People make sound to talk when air vibrates vocal cords in their throats. Different vibrations in the vocal cords make different sounds.
Picture: A close photo of a person mid-sentence, mouth open, talking to someone. The view tightens on the throat, where air vibrates the vocal cords, and the cords are shown moving quickly back and forth, fast and slow, to make different sounds.

Picture: A photo of two rubber bands, one thick and one thin, stretched over the open top of a shoebox on a table. A hand plucks the thin band, then the thick one, and each quivers as it sounds.

A vibrating object makes the air around it move. This is how sound travels. It moves outward in all directions. For example, when a tree crashes to the ground, the air around it vibrates. When the vibrating air reaches your ear, you hear the sound of a crashing tree. The vibrating air is called sound waves. Sound waves also travel through solids and liquids.
Picture: A photo of a big tree crashing down in a forest and hitting the ground hard. The air around the trunk shimmers and pushes outward in every direction, travelling as sound waves, and the waves move through the soil and through water too.

Different Sounds Not all sound is alike. Some sounds are loud. Others are quiet. The loudness of sound is called volume. The more air that vibrates, the louder the volume. For example, a lion’s roar vibrates more air than a buzzing bee’s wings. Sound also can be high or low. This is called pitch. Pitch depends on how fast something vibrates. Sirens vibrate quickly and have a high pitch. Bass drums vibrate slowly and have a low pitch.
Picture: A photo comparing loud and quiet, high and low: a lion with its mouth wide open, roaring and shaking lots of air, beside a tiny bee whose wings buzz softly. A siren screams high while a bass drum booms low and slow.

Ears and Sound The outside of your ear is shaped to collect sound waves. The sound waves move from outside the ear to inside the ear. Tiny bones inside vibrate when they are struck by sound waves. The vibrating bones send messages through nerves to your brain. Your brain then interprets the messages as noise, music, talking, or other sounds.
Picture: A close photo of a human ear. The cupped outer ear collects sound waves, which travel inside to the eardrum and the tiny bones, and those vibrating bones send messages along nerves to the brain, which reads them as noise, music or talking.
11
The inner parts of the ear are very sensitive. Very loud sounds can hurt your ears. Be careful around loud noises. Cover your ears with your hands, or wear coverings on your ears such as earphones or earplugs. Or simply move away from the loud sounds.

Louis Braille: A Man and His Method

12 pages · cartoon (famous person) · narrator: British man — a historian recounting the life and invention of Louis Braille
1
The Accident In 1809, Louis Braille was born in a small town in the country of France. One day at the age of three, Louis hurt himself. While playing in his father’s shoe workshop, he tried to make his own pair of shoes.
Picture: In a small town in France, baby Louis is born to his family. As a three-year-old boy, he plays in his father's shoe workshop, bending over leather and tools while trying to make his own pair of shoes.
2
Louis picked up his father’s sharp awl and bent over. The awl went into Louis’s eye. His hurt eye became sick. Before long, the sickness spread to his other eye. By age five, Louis could no longer see. He was blind.
Picture: Young Louis bends over in the shoe workshop, holding his father's sharp awl; the tool goes into his eye. His hurt eye becomes sick, and the sickness spreads to his other eye, leaving the five-year-old boy blind.
3
Louis went to school with children who could see. He learned by listening, but since he could not see, he could not read or write. Like many other parents of children who were blind back then, Louis’s parents feared for his future. However, when Louis was ten, they were able to send him to a special school in Paris.
Picture: Louis sits in a classroom with children who can see, listening carefully because he cannot read or write. At home, his parents look worried about his future. At ten, Louis travels to a special school for children who are blind in Paris.
4
A New Sort of School The school in Paris was for boys who were blind. It was one of the first such schools in the world. Louis learned many skills at this school. He learned how to weave branches together to make chairs. He learned how to sew leather to make slippers.
Picture: At the Paris school for boys who are blind, Louis learns practical skills. He weaves branches together to make a chair and sews leather to make slippers, working with other blind boys in one of the first such schools in the world.
5
Louis also learned how to read. He felt raised letters on a sheet of paper. The paper was pressed against copper wires shaped like letters. This made raised letters on the paper that fingers could feel. But the letters were difficult to read. Too many letters had similar shapes and felt the same. And the wire used to make these letters had to be shaped by people who could see. It seemed there had to be a better way for people who were blind to read.
Picture: Louis feels raised letters on a sheet of paper, learning to read by touch. The letters were pressed against copper wires shaped like letters, but many feel alike and are hard to tell apart. People who can see must shape the wires.
6
Night Writing Charles Barbier, a soldier, invented a form of night writing for France’s army. Soldiers often hid in the dark. They did not want to use a light because they did not want to be found. Night writing used twelve small raised dots to form each letter. It gave soldiers a way to read in the dark. It also allowed soldiers to send messages to one another without using light or making a noise. This code was too hard for most soldiers to learn, however. The army decided not to use it, so in 1821, Barbier took the system to Louis’s school.
Picture: Soldier Charles Barbier shows night writing: groups of twelve raised dots that soldiers can feel in darkness without using a light or making noise. In 1821, Barbier brings this dot system to Louis's school, where Louis examines the raised dots.
7
Louis believed a system of raised dots could be used to help people who are blind to read. He had been trying to create a touch system of his own for reading and writing. When he tried night writing, he knew it was the right idea but was also too difficult.
Picture: Louis sits with raised-dot samples at school, thinking about a touch system for reading and writing. He has been trying to create his own. Night writing gives him the right idea, but he sees that it is still too difficult.
8
The night-writing system did not allow a fingertip to touch a whole letter. Readers had to move their finger to feel the whole symbol. Night writing also did not include periods, question marks, music signs, or numbers. Louis asked Barbier to change his system to make it easier, but the soldier would not change it.
Picture: Louis moves a fingertip across a long twelve-dot symbol, unable to feel the whole letter at once. He notices that night writing has no periods, question marks, music signs, or numbers. Louis asks soldier Charles Barbier to make the system easier, but Barbier refuses to change it.
9
Louis’s Own System Over a few years, Louis made a shorter, easier form of this touch system. He used six dots instead of twelve to form each letter. It allowed a person’s fingertip to feel the entire symbol without having to move. This difference let people who are blind read much more quickly and easily.
Picture: Louis works at a table with small six-dot cells, designing a shorter, easier touch system. A person's fingertip can feel one whole symbol without moving. This change lets people who are blind read much more quickly and easily than with night writing.
10
At age fifteen, Louis shared this new system with his classmates and the school. Several years later, Louis added similar dot codes for music and math. Today, we call this form of written language braille. It is named after Louis Braille, who helped invent it. Now, people who are blind use a stylus and a slate to write braille. Dots are punched on the back of heavy paper with a stylus. A slate is used to keep the dots spaced correctly. Sentences are written from right to left. Then the paper is flipped over, and the sentences are read from left to right.
Picture: At fifteen, Louis shows his six-dot system to classmates and the school. Years later he adds dot codes for music and math. A person who is blind uses a stylus and slate to punch dots into heavy paper from right to left; then the paper is flipped to read left to right.
11
Braille can also be written using a Perkins Brailler, a typewriter-like machine. Braille gives people who are blind a way to both read and write. From Stylus to Smartphone.
Picture: A person who is blind sits at a Perkins Brailler, a typewriter-like machine, and presses keys to make raised braille dots. Beside the machine, a stylus, slate, and smartphone show how braille writing has moved from hand tools to modern devices.
12
A Loved Teacher Braille became a teacher at the school he attended as a child. He was loved by his students. He died in 1852, but his form of writing lives on. Even though Louis Braille became blind at a young age, he never gave up. Instead, he overcame great obstacles, which made it possible for people who are blind to read.
Picture: Adult Louis Braille teaches at the Paris school he once attended, surrounded by students who respect and love him. Years after his death in 1852, people who are blind still read and write using the system he created, because he never gave up.
