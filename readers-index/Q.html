An Invention That Changed the World For thousands of years, people used their muscles for work. They also used animals to help them. What Is Steam? Have you ever watched someone boil water in a pot on the stove? Sometimes you can see a little cloud above the pot. That’s steam. It can only be seen when it hits cooler air. Steam is a gas. People make steam do work by boiling water. They heat up the water in a gigantic pot called a boiler. They make the boiler hot by burning fuel, such as coal. Burning coal becomes hotter than the top of a stove—a lot hotter! The heat boils the water and makes steam. Steam takes up more room than liquid water. As the steam expands, it pushes the moveable parts of a machine. The movement of those parts creates power.

The First Real Steam Engines Almost 300 years ago, in 1712, Thomas Newcomen built a steam engine. Others had built steam engines, but Newcomen’s was the first to work really well. He used his steam engine to run a water pump, which pumped water out of coal mines. Miners could then reach more coal. The engine had one problem. It needed a large amount of coal to do a small amount of work.

Steam Engine Improvements In the 1760s, Scotsman James Watt improved Newcomen’s engine. He made the machine work with less coal. Watt also made other kinds of steam engines. He made one called the rotary (circular) steam engine. It could run all kinds of machines. This invention was one event that led to the Industrial Revolution, also known as the Age of Steam. The Age of Steam brought new ways to do work and new ways for people and goods to travel.

An explosion could injure or kill people.

Rain or underground water drips and flows into this bedrock and erodes it. This creates open spaces such as underground caves or caverns. Over time, these spaces get bigger. Eventually, the overburden may slowly drop down to form a sinkhole. Sometimes, though, sinkholes can open suddenly and swallow buildings, cars, and people. Two Types There are two main types of sinkholes. The first is a cover-subsidence sinkhole ( subside means to sink or settle). A cover-subsidence sinkhole forms when water wears a space in the underground bedrock. The overburden above the bedrock then shifts downward. Most sinkholes of this kind form slowly and may measure only a few yards across. The overburden may contain a lot of sand that easily slides into the underground space. These sinkholes often create a bowl shape on the surface. They may become ponds or wetlands if water has no way to drain out.

Introduction Snakes are reptiles, a class of animals that also includes lizards and turtles. Snakes have no limbs. They come in many lengths, patterns, and colors. There are several thousand types of snakes. They live in oceans, in trees, and on land, and they have adapted to survive in unusual ways.

Where Snakes Live Snakes live almost everywhere except Antarctica. Some places don’t have any snakes. Snakes do not live in the wild on islands such as Ireland, New Zealand, and Iceland. Some snakes in the wild may live up to twenty-five years. Biggest and Smallest The largest snake is the green anaconda. A full-grown green anaconda can be close to 10 meters (33 ft.) long and weigh more than 230 kilograms (507 lb.). One of the smallest snakes is the thread snake. Snake Traits Like all reptiles, snakes are cold-blooded. Their body temperature changes as the temperature around them changes. If a snake needs to warm up, it lies in the sun. If it needs to cool down, it rests in the shade. Snakes become much less active in winter. Some snakes that live through cold winters find a place underground or beneath a rock pile to stay warm. Another way snakes stay warm is by looping around each other to share body heat.
Picture: A snake lies stretched out in bright sunlight to warm up, while another rests in cool shade; nearby, snakes loop around each other in a shared coil, and one slips beneath a rock pile for warmth.
5
Snakes have a long backbone with ribs. Their backbone is made up of many separate bones. Because of this, they can move in many different ways. A snake’s body is covered in scales. The scales protect it. They also help keep the snake dry. The scales grow from the snake’s skin. Some snakes’ scales are colorful. Other scales help them blend in with their surroundings. Since snakes have no arms or legs, they use their scales and muscles to move. The scales on their belly tightly grip the surface beneath them. Their muscles help them move from side to side, or slither, along the ground. Some snakes even use their muscles and scales to climb trees.
Picture: A snake slithers smoothly across the ground, its colorful scales catching the light; nearby, another uses its muscles and belly scales to climb up a tree trunk, showing how it moves without arms or legs.
6
Snake scales look like a single sheet of skin. Snakes don’t really shed skin. Instead, they shed the outer layers of their scales. Snakes may shed up to four times per year. This is called molting. During molting, a snake rubs against a hard object such as a rock or log. Rubbing helps its skin stretch and split. Then the snake crawls out and leaves it behind. Snakes shed for several reasons. Young snakes shed more often than older snakes because they outgrow the skin faster. Unlike other animals, snakes’ skin does not stretch as they grow. Another reason snakes shed is to get rid of parasites that may grab on to their old skin.
Picture: A snake rubs its body against a rough rock, its old outer layer of scales splitting and peeling; nearby, a clear dry shed snake skin lies left behind on the ground beside a fallen log.
7
Scales also protect a snake’s eyes. Unlike many animals, snakes do not have eyelids. Instead, each eye is covered with a clear scale that protects it from drying out. Because of this, snakes can’t blink. Snakes do not have ears you can see. Instead, sound passes through the jawbone to the inner ear, which sends a signal to the snake’s brain. Snakes have developed a different way to hear sounds with this method.
Picture: A close view of a snake's head shows its eye covered by a clear scale and no eyelids; the snake's jaw rests against the ground, suggesting sound passing through the jawbone to its inner ear.
8
Another important feature of a snake is its tongue. A snake smells with its tongue. Its forked tongue picks up scents from the air. Scent particles stick to the tongue. The snake then pulls its tongue back into its mouth. When its tongue touches the roof of its mouth, a special organ helps to identify the scent. Some snakes are able to sense the heat of animals nearby. Pits near the snake’s eyes can sense the air temperature. This kind of sensing helps these snakes judge distances so they can hunt for prey in the dark.
Picture: A close-up shows a snake flicking out its forked tongue to pick up scents from the air; the tongue nears its mouth, and small pits near its eyes are visible as it hunts in the dark.
9
Hunting and Eating Snakes are carnivores, or meat eaters. They eat other animals in one of three ways. Some snakes use venom when biting their prey. Other snakes, called constrictors, squeeze their prey to death before swallowing it. Some snakes grab their prey with their jaws and swallow it alive. Most snakes’ upper and lower jaws are loosely connected. As a result, a snake can spread its jaws and open its mouth wide enough to swallow large objects. Snakes’ teeth are mainly made for catching and holding prey. Their fangs and other teeth curve backward, which pushes food toward their throat. They push their breathing tube out of their mouth so they can breathe as they swallow their prey whole. Snakes use strong muscles to move the prey into the stomach and intestines. Strong stomach acids turn the prey into liquid so the snake can digest it easier.
Picture: A snake with its jaws stretched wide begins to swallow a large prey whole, its backward-curving teeth holding the meal; nearby, another snake wraps its body tightly around prey as a constrictor on the ground.
10
How Snakes Are Born Most snakes that live in cold areas mate in the late spring or early summer. Snakes that live in warmer areas may mate at any time of year. The male snake usually leaves afterward. About two-thirds of all female snakes lay eggs in a safe place and then leave. Baby snakes grow inside the eggs. Most of them have sharp teeth that they use to cut through the egg to hatch. Once a baby snake leaves the egg, it must face many dangers and survive on its own. Some female snakes hold their eggs inside their body until they hatch. Then they give birth to live babies.
Picture: A female snake lays eggs in a safe hidden place and leaves; inside one egg, a baby snake with sharp teeth is ready to cut through the shell, while another female gives birth to live babies nearby.

Venomous Snakes Venom is a dangerous substance. Venom comes from the fangs on a snake’s jaws. Venom is made in sacs behind a snake’s eyes. Some fangs are hollow so the venom can flow through them from these sacs.
Picture: A close view of a venomous snake's head in the wild shows its fangs and the sacs behind its eyes where dangerous venom is made; its mouth opens slightly to reveal small clear hollow fangs.
12
Snakes use venom to defend and attack. Snakes usually inject more venom when they are defending themselves. The venom paralyzes their prey. Some inject venom and let the prey go. Then they track the prey until it dies. Other snakes use their teeth or body to hold on to prey until it dies. Then they eat it. Venom versus Poison Although commonly confused, venom and poison are not the same thing. Poison is absorbed through the skin, eaten, or breathed in, while venom is injected into the victim by means of a bite or sting.
Picture: A snake bites prey and then follows its trail through the undergrowth, waiting for the injected venom to paralyze it; nearby, another snake holds prey with its body until it stops moving before eating it.

What Are Hiccups? A hiccup is a muscle spasm, or twitch. The muscle that twitches is called the diaphragm. You use your diaphragm to breathe. Your diaphragm sits below your lungs and stretches across the bottom of your rib cage. Nerves carry signals from your brain to your diaphragm. To breathe in, your brain tells the diaphragm to tighten. When your diaphragm tightens, it moves downward, which pulls air into your lungs. When your brain tells your diaphragm to relax again, the muscle moves up to push air back out of your lungs.

Types of Memory Scientists often talk about two types of memory: working memory and long-term memory. Working memory is how we deal with information in the moment. The words you’re reading right now and the conversation you just had with a friend are part of your working memory. So is the music you were listening to on the way to school. The 1976 Viking mission was made up of two spacecraft. It was the first mission to land on the surface of Mars. Both spacecraft looked for signs of life by testing soil samples. In 1976, the Viking 2 rover landed on Mars and took samples of the surface. It sent information back to scientists on Earth. The scientists learned that the rusty red color was due to large amounts of iron, a metal, in the soil. Viking also reported no signs of organic matter—the remains of living things. Mars seemed to be a dry, desert planet.

Would I be weightless on Mars? No, but you would weigh less because you would experience less than half the gravity you do on Earth. Scientists were still puzzled, though. Canyons on Earth are formed by running water. Mars looked like Earth without water. In 1997, the Mars Pathfinder also found rocks that seemed to have been shaped by water. Could Mars have had water long ago?
Picture: A person stands on Mars and feels lighter, because gravity there is less than half of Earth’s. Nearby, rocks found by the 1997 Mars Pathfinder appear shaped by water. Since canyons on Earth are formed by running water, scientists wonder if Mars once had water.

Curiosity, a car-sized rover, launched in 2011. Its mission was to see if Mars could have supported small life- forms called microbes, and if humans could survive there someday! Looking for Answers In 2003, the European Space Agency (ESA) launched the Mars Express. This spacecraft would orbit Mars and collect information about the planet. Just one month after arriving at Mars, Mars Express found ice at the planet’s south pole. This meant there was water on Mars.
Picture: A car-sized rover named Curiosity travels toward Mars after launching in 2011, seeking signs that microbes could have lived there and whether humans could survive. The Mars Express spacecraft orbits the planet and finds ice at the south pole, meaning there is water on Mars.

How long is a day on Mars? A Martian day is just a bit longer than an Earth day—24 hours and 37 minutes. For the next several years, the Mars Express studied the planet’s south pole. In July 2018, European scientists said they had found a saltwater lake underneath about 2 kilometers (1 mi.) of ice on the surface of Mars. They think this lake is 20 kilometers (12 mi.) wide. Scientists think there may be other lakes, too, that might even contain life. In 2012 NASA sent the Curiosity rover to Mars. Curiosity tested samples of the soil and air on Mars. The air samples contained a gas called methane. On Earth, tiny living things called microbes make methane. Scientists wondered if the same might be true on Mars.

Scientists can’t say for sure if life really did or does exist on Mars. More Mars missions are planned for 2020 and beyond. These missions will send rovers to look for signs of life. They will also gather information to see if it might be possible for people to someday live there. Perhaps one day, Mars will be inhabited by intelligent beings—from Earth!
