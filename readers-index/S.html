Dogs at Work

13 pages · photo · narrator: Professional woman — An explainer would tell this aloud, describing how different working dogs do their jobs and why they are suited to them.

Did you know that some dogs aren’t just pets? These intelligent and obedient animals can hold important jobs that help people. Dogs at Work provides students with a comprehensive look at the different types of working dogs including service dogs, search-and-rescue dogs, and detection dogs.

Introduction You probably know that some dogs aren’t just pets—they hold jobs! Some dogs can work on farms, guarding or herding animals. Other dogs can pull sleds or help people hunt. Dogs have worked with people for thousands of years. What makes dogs such great workers? They learn fast, and they can also hear and smell better than we can. This means that dogs can do jobs that people can’t. Some of their jobs may surprise you.
Picture: On a farm, a dog guards or herds animals while another dog pulls a sled through snow and a third helps a person hunt. The dogs work alongside people, showing that they learn fast and can hear and smell better than humans. They are intelligent animals doing jobs people need.

Service and Therapy Dogs Many dogs work with people to help them feel better. These dogs are called therapy dogs. Therapy dogs comfort people and keep them calm. These dogs sometimes visit sick people in hospitals to cheer them up. Therapy dogs need to have a friendly personality. They are trained to react calmly to new places, smells, and people.
Picture: In a hospital room, a friendly therapy dog visits a sick person, standing calmly beside the bed while the person smiles. The dog has a gentle, relaxed manner and stays calm among new places, smells, and people. Its quiet comfort helps cheer the sick person up and keep them calm.

Other dogs help people with disabilities. These dogs are called service dogs. Service dogs can do many different jobs. For example, guide dogs help people who cannot see well. These dogs are trained to keep people from stepping into traffic. Guide dogs are also trained to lead their owner around objects. That way, their owner won’t trip or fall. Tennille the Guide Dog Guide dogs can be trained to help their owner have adventures. A guide dog named Tennille helped her owner hike in the wilderness. Tennille was the first guide dog trained to do this. Her owner, Trevor Thomas, is blind. Tennille helped Thomas over and around large rocks. She also looked for trail signs and led Thomas up to them. Thomas would then read the signs with his fingers. Thomas hopes that he and Tennille can encourage other blind people to follow their example.
Picture: A guide dog named Tennille leads her blind owner, Trevor Thomas, along a wilderness trail. She helps him over and around large rocks, then finds trail signs and leads him up to them. Thomas reads the signs with his fingers while Tennille stands patiently beside him.

Some service dogs help people who can’t hear well. These dogs listen for noises (like a doorbell), touch their owner, and move toward the noise. Some service dogs also help their owner move around. They pull wheelchairs, pick up dropped objects, press buttons, and open doors.
Picture: Inside a home, a service dog hears a doorbell and touches its owner, then moves toward the sound. Nearby, another service dog pulls a wheelchair, picks up a dropped object, presses a button, and opens a door for a person. The dogs help people who cannot hear well or move easily.

Some service dogs use their nose to help their owner. For example, dogs can help people with low blood sugar. These dogs can smell changes in blood and alert their owner to eat food or take medicine. Other service dogs can smell when a person is about to have a seizure. These sudden attacks cause body spasms and other problems. Service dogs can warn the person before it happens. Other dogs work with children who have food allergies. Some children can’t eat peanuts. Service dogs can help them by smelling hidden peanuts in food.
Picture: A service dog sniffs near its owner and alerts them to a change in blood sugar, while another dog warns a person before a seizure. A third dog smells hidden peanuts in food for a child with a food allergy. The dogs use their noses to help keep people safe and healthy.

Search-and-Rescue Dogs Some dogs work to find people who are lost, hurt, or trapped. These dogs are called search-and-rescue (SAR) dogs. Many SAR dogs use their powerful nose to find people who need help. Dogs can be trained to do this in two different ways. The first way is through “tracking”— putting their nose to the ground to follow a scent. Tracking dogs can follow a missing person when the scent is fresh. They need to work quickly, before the scent fades.
Picture: A search-and-rescue dog puts its nose to the ground and follows a fresh scent along a trail. The tracking dog moves quickly before the scent fades, searching for a lost, hurt, or trapped person. It works with its powerful nose to find someone who needs help.
8
The second way that dogs can search for missing people is by sniffing the air instead of the ground. These “air-scent” dogs often work in a big search area. Using only their nose, they can find skiers trapped under 5 meters (16 ft.) of snow. They can also find lost hikers. They can even find people trapped in buildings that have fallen down.
Picture: An air-scent search-and-rescue dog sniffs the air in a wide search area. The dog finds a skier trapped under deep snow, a lost hiker, and a person trapped in a fallen building. It works using only its nose, moving through the search area to locate people who need help.
9
Other SAR dogs work in the water instead of on land. Water rescue dogs can be trained to pull drowning people to safety. They can find people underwater and pull small boats to land. Water rescue dogs need to be strong swimmers. For this reason, big dogs like Newfoundlands make good water rescue dogs. Newfoundland dogs have waterproof fur and webbed toes, and they love to swim. They are also much stronger than human lifeguards. A 68-kilogram (150 lb.) Newfoundland can pull ten people to safety at once!
Picture: A large Newfoundland water rescue dog swims strongly in the water, using its waterproof fur and webbed toes. It pulls a drowning person to safety and helps pull a small boat to land. The powerful dog can find people underwater and is strong enough to pull many people at once.

Detection Dogs Some dogs work as detectives. These dogs are called detection dogs. They use their sense of smell to help people find things that are hidden. Police use detection dogs often. They use arson dogs when a building has burned down. It may be hard to find out whether someone started the fire on purpose. Arson dogs sniff for chemicals that might have been used to start the fire.
Picture: At a burned building, an arson detection dog sniffs for chemicals that might have been used to start the fire. The dog uses its sense of smell to find hidden clues for police. It works as a detective, helping people learn whether someone started the fire on purpose.
11
Detection dogs are also used at airports and border crossings. These dogs search for objects that cannot be brought into a country or onto a plane. It’s usually illegal to bring fresh fruit or live animals across a border without permission. Detection dogs can find these things hidden in people’s bags. Police also use detection dogs to find criminals. Other detection dogs work outside the world of crime. For example, some dogs are trained to search for termites in houses and other buildings. Termites damage buildings by eating wood. They are hard for humans to find, but termite dogs can sniff them out.
Picture: At an airport or border crossing, a detection dog sniffs people’s bags for hidden fresh fruit or live animals that cannot be brought in without permission. Another detection dog searches a house for termites that damage wood. Police also use detection dogs to find criminals. The dogs use their noses to find things people cannot easily see.

Detection dogs may even be able to help doctors in the future. Some dogs, for example, seem to be able to smell cancer in humans. Scientists are studying these dogs to see if they can help doctors find cancer quickly in their patients. Odd Jobs Dogs quickly learn to do even unusual jobs. For example, a dog named Piper worked with his owner at an airport in Michigan. His job was to chase wild birds out of the path of planes on runways. Another group of dogs had an unusual job at an oceanside baseball park in San Francisco. These dogs jumped into the ocean to get balls that were hit into the water. The program is now over, but for a time, baseball fans loved to collect these “Splash Hit” balls.

Conclusion Dogs help us do many types of jobs. As our work changes and grows, so do the jobs we give to dogs. From sniffing out cancer cells to pulling a swimmer to safety, dogs help humans in many ways. Their strength, friendliness, intelligence, and powerful senses make dogs wonderful workers.

History of Kites Thousands of years ago, the first toy kites were named after the kite bird. This large, graceful bird has a very wide wingspan. Many early kites were shaped like birds, but people kept experimenting with ways to make kites. Slowly, kites became more elaborate. They took on certain features and flight patterns. Introduction Earth’s tropical oceans contain places of wonder and beauty known as coral reefs. These huge undersea communities are home to thousands of living things. Day and night, animals hunt, eat, and avoid being eaten. They depend on each other for food, shelter, and safety. Almost one-fourth of all ocean plants and animals live around coral reefs. Fish, sea turtles, sea stars, and many other animals make reefs their home. Visitors include seabirds and dolphins.
Picture: In clear warm tropical water, a busy reef crowded with fish, a sea turtle and sea stars among the corals; above, dolphins break the surface while seabirds fly over the open ocean, showing the animals that live around and visit the reef.

What Is Coral? At first glance, coral may look like oddly shaped rocks or plants. In fact, corals are tiny animals called polyps (PAH-lips). Most polyps are smaller than a pea. One branch or mound of coral can have thousands of polyps. Each polyp has a tubelike body and a mouth with tiny tentacles. Polyps are eating machines. At night, they open their tentacles to catch tiny animals. Hundreds of kinds of corals live in the ocean. Most are one of two kinds. Hard corals, also known as stony corals, form hard skeletons. Over time, many millions of these hard skeletons build up and form reefs.
Picture: A close-up of one coral branch at night, showing tiny polyps smaller than a pea, each with a tubelike body and a mouth ringed by small tentacles reaching into the dark water to catch tiny animals; beside it, a hard stony coral skeleton.

Most stony corals live together with algae (AL-jee). Algae are simple cousins of plants. They supply food for the corals and for many reef fish. Soft corals can bend with the currents. They often look like trees, bushes, or other plants. Algae live in the bodies of some, but not all, soft corals.
Picture: Two kinds of coral side by side: a stony coral living with green algae, and soft corals swaying and bending with the current, shaped like trees and bushes on the reef, with reef fish swimming past them.

A Busy Underwater Community Most people think of brightly colored fish when they think about coral reefs—and with good reason. Thousands of kinds of fish live on reefs. The reefs provide them with food. Reefs also protect them from animals that hunt them for food. Shrimp, lobsters, and crabs also find food at reefs. Sea stars catch small animals that hide among the coral branches. Sea anemones, which look like flowers, wait for their next meal to drift past.
Picture: A crowded reef scene: brightly colored fish swimming among corals, a shrimp, a lobster and a crab finding food on the reef, a sea star reaching among coral branches, and a flower-like sea anemone with tentacles waiting for food to drift past.
6

The largest rainforest on Earth is the Amazon rainforest in South America. Rainforests like the Amazon are important because they are one of the most diverse habitats on Earth. They are home to people, including Indigenous tribes such as the Yanomami (yah-noh-MAH-mee). They are also home to 80% of the world’s plant and animal species. Without the essential habitats rainforests offer, the Earth could face the decline or extinction of much of its wildlife. Rainforests also help clean the Earth’s air and water and regulate its climate. They are sometimes called natural air filters because rainforest trees can remove pollution from the air and release clean oxygen. Rainforests provide a similar filtering effect to Earth’s waterways. Without rainforests, life on Earth would not be the same.

Living for Today and Tomorrow The people of the Yanomami tribe, one of the world’s oldest cultures, call the Amazon rainforest home. Indigenous tribes like the Yanomami have lived in the Amazon for more than five thousand years. The Yanomami live in what is known today as Brazil and Venezuela, although the Yanomami lived there long before those countries ever existed. The Yanomami have collected thousands of years of knowledge about the Amazon rainforest. Their lifestyle is dependent on living in harmony with the rainforest’s many species of plants and animals. Because the Yanomami lifestyle relies on the rainforest, they are careful to never overuse resources or harm the environment. Yanomami Territory.

The Yanomami live in a way that is sustainable, which means the way they live today is designed to leave a healthy rainforest for tomorrow. They avoid cutting down more trees, picking more fruit, and hunting more animals than they need to survive. They live this way in hopes that their children and grandchildren will still have the trees, fruit, and animals they need—and that the rainforest’s ecosystem will remain healthy. Rainforests and the Dangers As rainforests shrink, the quality of Earth’s air and water are affected. So are the habitats of hundreds of plant and animal species. Yet, deforestation, or the removal of trees, continues to be problematic for the Amazon and other rainforests.

Life in the Rainforest For thousands of years, the Amazon rainforest has given the Yanomami all they need to survive. They have not had to rely on trade with other nations for the goods they require. Like many other Indigenous people of South America, the Yanomami believe the spiritual world is very important. They use knowledge of local plants and animals to get closer to this world. Beyond that, they use the forest to meet their daily needs, including shelter, food, and clothing. Do You Know? Yanomami can be spelled in different ways: Yanoama, Yanomama, and Yanomamo.

Yanos and Community The Yanomami live in big, circular huts called yanos. They build them with wooden poles, vines, and thatched leaves. Viewing a yano from above, it would look like a big circle with a hole cut out in the middle. The center of the circle, open to the sky, is where adults meet and children play. An entire Yanomami village often lives in one yano, with many families sharing the same roof. Instead of living in separate rooms, each family has its own area where family members eat and sleep. Each family has its own fire pit for cooking, and each person has their for sleep.

Inside, many families share the roof, each with its own eating and sleeping area and fire pit for cooking.

Gathering and Gardening The Yanomami know the rainforest well. They know which herbs to use for different illnesses; they know which plants are edible and which are poisonous. They gather wild foods such as fruit, nuts, roots, and insects. They also plant gardens in the rainforest. First they cut and burn the trees on small sections of land, then plant sugar cane, plantains, sweet potatoes, manioc, and more. To avoid harming the land, the Yanomami move their garden every few years and allow the old garden to grow back into rainforest. When the Yanomami do not have enough food from gathering and gardening, they set out on a wayumi. A wayumi is a trek through the rainforest during which the entire village travels for days or weeks while searching for food. On wayumis, the Yanomami eat roots, out of bark, and sleep under leaves to keep dry.

Hunting and Fishing Yanomami hunters hunt using longbows and wooden arrows with bird feathers attached to help them fly straight. Sometimes they dip their arrowheads in poison. Hunters walk barefoot through the rainforest looking for anteaters, tapirs, and other animals. When hunters kill animals, they share the meat with the entire village. The Yanomami use a variety of methods to catch fish. Some shoot arrows at fish in the river, while others skim baskets through the water. The Yanomami also know how to fish with the poisonous ayori-toto vine. They cut the vine and slide it through the water, and the poison drips into the water stunning nearby fish. The fish then float to the surface, and the Yanomami scoop them up.

Yanomami Fashion The clothing choices people make often depend on where they live, how they live, and what materials are available. The Yanomami get everything they need from the rainforest— and use it with the rainforest’s conditions in mind. The rainforest is often hot and rainy, so the Yanomami dress lightly to stay cool and mobile. Men often wear loincloths, women often wear fringed aprons, and children tend to wear little or no clothing for their own comfort in the heat. The Yanomami often paint their bodies with different colors. They use red dyes from seeds, purple dyes from palm fruit, and black powder from charcoal. Many of their designs involve patterns with dots and lines.

Some Yanomami tattoo rather than paint their markings on, and their tattoos have a variety of different cultural purposes. Tattoos can serve as a link to the spiritual world. They can also demonstrate a person’s accomplishments or mark their transition into adulthood. Some Yanomami people pierce their noses, lips, and ears. They use small wooden sticks as nose and lip rings and bird feathers for earrings. They also wear other jewelry made from the rainforest, such as necklaces of seeds and armbands of fragrant leaves. Sometimes they crown their heads with small, white hawk feathers.

Yanomami Land Today About 20,000 Yanomami remain in the Amazon today. They live similar to the way their ancestors did, still hunting, gathering food, sleeping under yanos, and fashioning their clothing from materials in the rainforest. But in the last fifty years, more outsiders have arrived—and they do not share the Yanomami’s knowledge of or respect for the rainforest. These outsiders travel by motorized boats, trucks, and airplanes, and they come looking to benefit from the fertile and mineral-rich lands. They mine, hunt, fish, and cut down trees in ways that are not sustainable. Unlike the Yanomami, they are not careful to keep the rainforest healthy. In the 1970s, the number of outsiders coming to the rainforest increased. Many of the outsiders were garimpeiros, a Portuguese word for “wildcat gold miners.” The garimpeiros entered Yanomami land hoping to strike it rich; they cared more about finding gold than the harm they could do to the rainforest.

Gold-mining is one of the largest threats to the Yanomami and the rainforest. Unfortunately, the appeal of getting rich has increased illegal mining. The consequences of gold-mining have devastated the rainforest in many ways, not only through the loss of trees, but also through pollution caused by toxic chemicals entering major waterways and the soil. Between the effects of the outsiders and the destructive nature of forest fires caused by global warming, life for the Yanomami and the Amazon rainforest itself is in great danger. If the rainforest continues to be destroyed, the whole world will suffer alongside the Yanomami, since the rainforest helps maintain a healthy global climate. The Yanomami must deal with outsiders and the diseases and the pollution of the forest that supports lifestyle.

Conclusion The Yanomami culture is rich with centuries of knowledge about the Amazon rainforest and the discipline and skills needed to live sustainably. Although many people have never interacted directly with the Yanomami, the problems they are facing have captured attention worldwide. The Yanomami have been depicted in books, magazines, movies, and art. Many who study their culture want to help others learn to prevent further negative impacts on the Yanomami and the Amazon rainforest. The Amazon rainforest is important to everyone on Earth. It produces a lot of the world’s oxygen, affects the world’s climate, supports a large variety of plant and animal life, and of course—is home to the Yanomami people.

What Are Butterflies and Moths? Butterflies and moths are insects. Like all insect species, they are invertebrates, which means they have no backbone. Instead, they have a hard skin, called an exoskeleton, that protects their soft insides. They also have six legs, a body divided into three parts, two antennae, and two compound eyes. Their wings are covered with scales, which are like fine, flat hairs. These scales protect the wings from getting too wet and help them hold in heat. Butterflies cannot fly if they are too cold. They often sit in the sun to warm up before flying.

When eggs hatch, caterpillars come out. This begins the larval stage of the butterfly and moth life cycle. Caterpillars have chewing mouth parts and can eat 27,000 times their body weight during their lifetime. Their egg case is often their first meal, followed by the leaf or flower they’re on, and then the rest of the plant. A caterpillar’s job is to eat and grow. As it grows, its skin becomes tight and breaks away. A new skin grows in its place to allow the caterpillar to get bigger. This is called molting. A caterpillar molts four or five times during its life. The caterpillar is fully grown after about two weeks.

N PACIFIC OCEAN Some butterflies and moths that live in cold places migrate to warmer places before winter begins. The best known butterfly that migrates is the monarch butterfly. Monarchs in North America begin their long journey south when it begins to cool off in late summer or fall. Monarchs living west of the Rocky Mountains fly to several places on the California coast. Monarchs living east of the Rockies fly as far as 4,800 kilometers (2,982 mi) to Mexico. When they reach warmer places, millions of them rest in trees through the winter. In spring, they migrate north again in search of plants on which to lay their eggs. No one knows how monarchs find their way.
