Ships and Boats

12 pages · drawing · narrator: Professor — A historian would tell it, tracing how boats changed over time and helped people explore the world.
1
Introduction Before there were cars, trains, or airplanes, boats were the fastest way to travel. They helped to shape history, leading explorers to new lands and spreading new ideas around the world. Ships and boats have changed a lot over the years but still have much in common with the earliest designs.
Picture: An early wooden boat carries explorers across open water toward a distant new land, while a later boat travels nearby on the same water, showing how ships changed over time yet still share the same basic design.
2
Early Ships and Boats The first boats were carved from single wooden logs. These dugout canoes were used in rivers, lakes, and oceans around the world. People used long poles, paddles, or oars to move the canoes through the water. Paddles and oars are long pieces of wood with broad, flat ends. They push against the water to move the boat forward. Later, people began to make boats with wooden frames. They covered the frame with bark, reeds, or animal skins to keep out the water.
Picture: On a river, lake, or ocean, people paddle a dugout canoe carved from a single wooden log, using long paddles with broad, flat ends; nearby, a wooden frame boat is covered with bark, reeds, or animal skins.
3
In the South Pacific, people were building boats that could sail well beyond the islands where they lived. People took two canoes and connected them using large wooden poles. This style of boat was called a catamaran. The catamaran could travel very fast. It also allowed safe travel from one island to another.
Picture: In the South Pacific, people build a fast catamaran by joining two canoes with large wooden poles. The double-hulled boat travels safely over the water from one island to another, carrying people beyond the islands where they live.
4
Some frame ships, such as galleys, were much larger than log boats. Thousands of years ago, many groups of people around the Mediterranean Sea used galleys for trade and warfare. Galleys were frame ships covered with wood that carried many rowers. The largest galleys had hundreds of rowers sitting on several decks. Many galleys also had sails made from strong cloth. Sails could be used when the wind was blowing in the direction the ship was traveling.
Picture: On the Mediterranean Sea, a large wooden galley carries many rowers sitting on several decks, pulling long oars. A strong cloth sail rises above the frame ship, ready to catch the wind as the galley travels for trade and warfare.

The Vikings used wooden-framed longships to cross the sea to raid towns and villages on other coasts. Like galleys, longships had sails as well as oars. The Vikings could land by surprise anywhere along the coast, something that helped them in battle.
Picture: A Viking longship with a wooden frame crosses the sea, using both a sail and many oars. Vikings aboard the longship approach a coast, ready to land by surprise at towns and villages, a move that helped them in battle.
6
Around 500 years ago, larger sailing ships began to be built. These sailing ships were able to cross the ocean. Famous explorers such as Ferdinand Magellan used sailing ships to travel from Europe to North and South America. Magellan was also the first captain to sail completely around the world.
Picture: Ferdinand Magellan stands on a large sailing ship crossing the ocean from Europe toward North and South America. The ship's cloth sails fill with wind as it travels, and Magellan becomes the first captain to sail completely around the world.

Modern Ships and Boats When steam engines were invented, they were used in many boat and ship designs. Steamships did not depend on the wind for power. They could sail into the wind or even when there was no wind at all. Steamboats using paddlewheels became an important way to move quickly up and down rivers.
Picture: A steamboat with a paddlewheel moves quickly along a river, powered by a steam engine rather than the wind. Nearby, a steamship travels on open water, able to sail into the wind or even when no wind blows at all.
8
Today, many boats are made of metal and plastic. Many ships and boats use electricity to turn their propellers. The propellers push the boat through the water. Some modern ships are very large. Cruise ships are like floating hotels. They can carry thousands of people all around the world. Huge tankers carry oil and gas over the ocean.
Picture: Today, boats made of metal and plastic use electricity to turn propellers that push them through the water. A huge cruise ship, like a floating hotel, carries thousands of people around the world, while a massive tanker carries oil and gas across the ocean.
9
Some boats and ships are built to do special work. Tugboats push and pull larger ships in places where they can’t move themselves. Fireboats help put out fires on other ships. Ferryboats carry cars and people.
Picture: Special workboats are shown on the water: a tugboat pushes and pulls a larger ship where it cannot move itself, a fireboat helps put out fires on another ship, and a ferryboat carries cars and people across the water.
10
Many countries use ships and boats, large and small, to defend their coasts. Submarines hide under the surface of the water. Aircraft carriers serve as floating runways and allow planes and helicopters to fly anywhere in the world.
Picture: A country's ships and boats defend its coast, large and small. A submarine hides under the surface of the water, while an aircraft carrier serves as a floating runway, allowing planes and helicopters to fly anywhere in the world from its deck.

Although large, modern boats with propellers are everywhere today, people still use smaller boats for fishing, fun, and sport. It is almost impossible to look at a river, lake, or ocean and not see a variety of boats. Many smaller boats still use paddles, oars, or sails, just as the earliest boats did.
Picture: On a river, lake, or ocean, people use a variety of smaller boats for fishing, fun, and sport. Some boats still use paddles, oars, or sails, just as the earliest boats did, while large modern boats with propellers can also be seen nearby.

Conclusion Ships and boats have played an important part in human history. For thousands of years, they have allowed people to explore their world. They are still an important way to move quickly over the water.

Introduction Snakes are reptiles. There are several thousand kinds of snakes. They live in oceans, in trees, and on land, and they survive in unusual ways.

Snake Traits Snakes are cold-blooded. Their body temperature changes with the temperature around them. Snakes find sunshine to stay warm. They find shade to stay cool. During winter, some snakes stay underground or under rock piles to stay warm. Snakes also rest in groups to share body heat.
Picture: Snakes are shown warming themselves in sunshine and cooling in shade; some rest underground or under rock piles during winter, while a group of snakes rests together to share body heat, their bodies close to the ground.
4
Snakes have long backbones. Their backbones have many separate bones, which lets them move in many ways. A snake’s body is covered in scales. The scales protect it. They also help keep the snake dry. The scales grow from its skin. Snakes use their scales and muscles to move. The scales on their belly grab the surface beneath them. Their muscles help them move along the ground. Some snakes even use their muscles and scales to climb trees.
Picture: A close view shows a snake’s long backbone made of many separate bones, and its body covered in scales; another snake uses belly scales to grip the ground while its muscles bend and push it forward, and one climbs a tree.
5
Snake scales look like a single sheet of skin. Snakes don’t shed skin. Instead, they shed the outer layers of their scales up to four times per year. This is called molting. When molting, snakes rub against something like a rock or log. This helps their outer layer stretch and split. Young snakes shed more because they grow fast. Snakes also shed to get rid of pests on their scales.
Picture: A snake rubs against a rough rock and a log, its outer scale layers stretching and splitting as it molts; a smaller young snake nearby also sheds scales, and loose old skin is left behind.
6
Snakes have clear scales instead of eyelids. The scales keep their eyes from drying out. Snakes cannot blink. Snakes do not have ears you can see. Instead, sound passes through their jaws to their inner ears, which sends the sound to their brain.
Picture: A close view of a snake’s eye shows a clear scale instead of an eyelid, keeping the eye from drying out; the snake cannot blink, and another view shows its jaw area where sound passes inward toward its inner ears.
7
Another important feature of a snake is its tongue. Snakes use their tongue to smell. Smells in the air stick to their tongue. They identify the smell using special sacs behind their nose. Some snakes can sense the heat of animals nearby. Pits near the snake’s eyes sense heat. These snakes can tell how far away something is in the dark when they hunt.
Picture: A snake flicks its tongue into the air to catch smells, then uses special sacs behind its nose to identify them; a close view shows heat pits near its eyes as it hunts in the dark and judges distance.
8
Hunting and Eating Snakes are meat eaters. Some snakes inject venom when biting prey. Other snakes squeeze prey to death. Some snakes swallow prey alive. Most snakes’ jaws are loosely connected. This lets snakes open their mouth wide to swallow large prey. Snakes’ fangs and other teeth point backward, helping push food toward their throat. Snakes have a breathing tube that they push out of their mouth. This lets them breathe when they swallow prey. Muscles move it to the stomach. Stomach acids turn prey into liquid so the snake can digest it easier.
Picture: A snake opens its loosely connected jaws wide to swallow large prey, while backward-pointing teeth push food toward its throat; another injects venom when biting prey, one squeezes prey, and another pushes a breathing tube out of its mouth.
9
How Snakes Are Born Most female snakes that live in cold areas lay eggs in late spring or early summer. Snakes that live in warmer areas may mate at any time of year. Baby snakes may grow sharp teeth to cut through the eggs. Once they hatch, they must survive on their own and face many dangers. Some female snakes keep eggs inside their body until they hatch. Then they give birth to live babies.
Picture: A female snake in a cold area lays eggs in late spring or early summer; babies use sharp teeth to cut through the eggs and then face dangers on their own, while another female keeps eggs inside until live babies are born.
10
Venomous Snakes Venom is a dangerous liquid. Some snakes inject venom when they bite with their fangs. Venom is made in sacs behind the snake’s eyes. It flows through the snake’s fangs from these sacs.
Picture: A close view of a venomous snake’s head shows its fangs and the sacs behind its eyes where venom is made; the dangerous liquid flows from those sacs through the fangs when the snake bites.
11
Snakes use venom to defend and attack. Snakes usually inject more venom when biting in defense. The venom paralyzes their prey. Some snakes inject venom and let the prey go. Then they follow the prey until it dies. Other snakes use their teeth or body to hold on to prey until it dies. Then they eat it. Venom versus Poison.
Picture: A snake bites in defense and injects venom, then follows prey it has let go until the prey dies; another snake uses its teeth or body to hold on to prey until it dies, then eats it.
12
Conclusion Snakes are amazing hunters. They have survived almost everywhere. Snakes are unusual animals that play an important role in our environment.
Picture: Several snakes are shown as hunters in different natural surroundings, moving through land, trees, and water; one snake catches prey while others move quietly through their habitats, showing how they survive almost everywhere and play a role in the environment.
