Different kinds of fires are stopped in one of three different ways. One is to remove the fuel, and a second way is to smother the fire by removing its oxygen. Magnetism

19 pages · photo · narrator: Clear adult man — A science explainer would tell this aloud, guiding readers through how magnetism works and where it is used.
1
Look around your home and try to find a magnet. A magnet is a metal object that sticks to other metals. You might have some magnets among your toys. There are probably magnets on your refrigerator. But did you know that there may be hundreds of magnets in your home? You cannot see most of them. But they are inside your television, telephone, and stereo. They are inside electric motors that power your blender and hair dryer. Computers are full of magnets. And all the electricity that runs these things comes from magnetism.
Picture: A home scene shows a magnet on a refrigerator and toy magnets among toys. Cutaway views reveal hidden magnets inside a television, telephone, stereo, blender, hair dryer, and computer. Electricity and magnetism are linked to these everyday objects.
2
Magnetism and Magnets Magnetism is an invisible force. A force is anything that pushes, pulls, or moves an object. Magnetism is a special force that only pulls on some metals, such as iron. A magnet will not push or pull plastic or tin. Magnetism flows in one direction through a magnet, no matter what shape the magnet is. It flows in one end and out the other. The ends of a magnet are called magnetic poles. Every magnet has a north and a south pole. The force flows out the north pole and back in the south pole.
Picture: A magnet attracts iron objects but leaves plastic and tin untouched. A visible flow of magnetic force travels in one end of the magnet and out the other. At the ends are a north pole and a south pole, showing the force's direction through the magnet.
3
You can use two magnets to feel magnetism flowing. The north pole of one magnet will stick to the south pole of the other. Now, try to push the two north poles together. It feels almost like trying to connect two hoses that are both spraying water. The magnetic force pushes the north poles apart, because the magnetic forces are flowing against each other. Two north poles will always repel each other. So will two south poles.
Picture: Two magnets are held so a north pole meets a south pole and they stick together. Then two north poles are pushed toward each other and resist, like two hoses spraying water against each other. The magnetic forces flow against each other, so the north poles repel.
4
The Earth’s iron core is magnetic. The whole planet acts like a giant magnet. If you dangle a magnet by a string, one pole will point north and the other will point south. This is exactly of a magnet their names. Do You Know? Earth’s north and south magnetic poles are not quite at the true North and South Poles. The north magnetic pole is in northern Canada. The south magnetic pole is in the Pacific Ocean south of Australia. Since Earth’s core moves around, the magnetic poles move, too. They can even flip completely around. If this happened today, your compass needle would point south!
Picture: Earth appears as a giant magnet with its iron core inside. A magnet hanging from a string points one pole north and the other south. The north magnetic pole lies in northern Canada; the south magnetic pole is in the Pacific Ocean south of Australia. These magnetic poles move and can even flip.
5
The magnetic force flows out the north pole of the magnet. Then, it loops around outside the magnet and flows back in the south pole. Magnetism flows through space in lines of force. The invisible shape formed by the lines of force around the magnet is called the magnetic field.
Picture: Magnetic force flows out of a magnet's north pole, loops around through space, and returns into the south pole. The invisible lines of force form a curved shape around the magnet. This surrounding shape is the magnetic field.
6

About Trees

1
Introduction Trees are the tallest living plants. Redwood trees can grow as tall as a 30-story building. Giant sequoias can weigh as much as 3,000 large pickup trucks, making them one of the heaviest living things. Trees also live a long time. Many trees alive today were full-grown long before the United States became a country. But these massive trees share something with all plants—they make their own food.
Picture: A grove of towering redwood trees seen from the forest floor, trunks rising out of sight. Beside a colossal giant sequoia, several large pickup trucks sit parked to show its enormous bulk. Beyond them, an old, thick-trunked tree stands in a green woodland, sunlight falling through the high canopy.
2
Leaves Imagine being able to make your own food without cooking or even going to a restaurant! Leaves make food for trees by changing energy from sunlight into food. This important work is done by chlorophyll (KLOR-uh-fill), the green coloring in leaves. Leaves come in all shapes and sizes. Most deciduous (dee-SIJ-oo-us) trees have wide, thin leaves, while most conifers have needlelike leaves. Conifers keep their needles through all seasons. Only the oldest needles fall to the ground. Deciduous trees lose their leaves every fall.
Picture: A close view of green leaves on a branch, bright sunlight shining through them. Next to it, wide thin leaves of a deciduous tree spread flat, while a conifer's needlelike leaves cluster along a twig. A few of the oldest brown needles lie fallen on the ground beneath the conifer.

In the fall, the leaves of deciduous trees show their true colors. These true colors are yellows, oranges, and browns, which hide under green chlorophyll all spring and summer. We see these colors in the fall after leaves stop making chlorophyll.
Picture: Autumn woodland scene: the leaves of deciduous trees turned yellow, orange and brown, filling the branches. Fallen leaves scatter across the ground below the trunks, and a few green leaves still cling nearby, showing the colors that were hidden during spring and summer.
4
Branches Branches are the arms that hold up a tree’s leaves. Branches spread leaves out to get as much sunlight as they can. The leaves give shade to other living things on sunny days. Branches start out as twigs, then they grow thicker each year. As a tree grows, its bark cracks open so the branches and trunk can expand. New bark is always growing under the old, ready to protect the tree. If new bark gets stripped away, a tree can die.
Picture: A tree's spreading branches reaching outward, holding leaves up to the sunlight, casting shade on the ground where small plants and a person rest. Nearer the trunk, thin twigs and thicker branches are visible, and the bark shows cracks opening where the trunk and branches have expanded.
5
Trunk Tubes in the tree’s trunk carry water from the roots up to the leaves. They also carry sap, or food, down from the leaves to the roots. These tubes are like highways, carrying traffic back and forth. They are close to the outside of the tree, just under the bark.
Picture: A cut section of a tree trunk showing the layers just under the bark, with narrow tubes running up and down the wood. Water travels upward from the roots toward the leaves, and sap moves back down, the tubes packed close to the outside of the trunk.
6
A tree doesn’t need the middle of its trunk to live. The middle is made of rings of old growth, not active highways. That’s why a tree can keep growing even if the middle is hollowed out after a lightning strike. All it needs is enough bark to protect its highways.
Picture: A storm-damaged tree with a hollow, blackened middle where lightning struck, the inside open and empty. Around the hollow, living bark and outer wood remain intact, and green leaves still grow on the branches above, showing the tree continuing to grow.
7
Roots Trees need soil to keep growing. Roots are part of the highways. They soak up water and nutrients from the soil. Roots also keep the soil in place when it rains. Without the roots of trees and plants, soil washes away. Trees are stuck in the soil because their roots reach deep into the ground. It’s a good thing they can make their own food, since they can’t move to get it.
Picture: Tree roots spreading down into dark soil, gripping the earth. Rain falls on a slope where roots of trees and smaller plants hold the soil in place instead of letting it wash away, and the rooted trunk stands fixed in the ground.
8
Sap Leaves use water plus sunlight and air to make food for the tree. This food is in the form of liquid sugars. Sometimes people eat some kinds of tree sugars for food. You’ve probably had tree sugar for breakfast—pouring it over your pancakes or waffles. Have you guessed what tree sugar this is? It’s maple syrup, and it comes from the food, or sap, of a sugar maple tree.
Picture: A sugar maple tree with its trunk tapped, sap dripping into a bucket. In the foreground a breakfast table shows a stack of pancakes and waffles with golden maple syrup poured over them, the bottle of syrup standing beside the plate.
9
Seeds Deciduous trees grow flowers, which make seeds that are inside fruit or nuts. Conifers make cones instead of flowers. The cones do the same job for conifers that flowers do on other trees. They make seeds to grow more trees. Each seed holds its own supply of food to keep it alive until it’s ready to sprout.
Picture: Branches of a deciduous tree carrying flowers that have become fruit and nuts, while a conifer nearby holds woody cones among its needles. Seeds lie exposed inside an opened cone and inside a cut fruit, each with its own store of food.
10
Growing How does a tall tree grow from a small seed? The seed soaks up water until it can send out a root. With more water, leaves inside the seed case grow until they push up and out. Once this has happened, we say the seed has sprouted. Now the sprout can make its own food and no longer needs the seed’s store of energy. The seed leaves will make food for the new sprout.
Picture: A seed resting on damp soil, split open with a small root pushing downward into the earth. Above it, tiny seed leaves push up out of the seed case, and a small green sprout stands with its first leaves unfolded in the light.

Trees are homes and food for birds and other animals. Look to see which animals make homes in trees near your home. Some sap, fruit, and trees. Others eat bark and leaves.
